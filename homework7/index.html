1. Modes of convergence

Let X1, X2, … be random variables and write Sn = X1 + ⋯ + Xn, X̄n = Sn/n. For iid variables, μ and σ2 denote the common mean and variance.

In probability: P(|Xn − X| ≥ ε) → 0.
Almost surely: P(lim Xn = X) = 1.
In mean square (L2): E[(Xn − X)2] → 0.
In distribution: Fn(x) → F(x) at continuity points of F.

The first three control the same pair (Xn, X) in different ways: a single large draw, the whole realized tail, and average squared error. Distributional convergence controls only the marginal law; Xn and X need not be defined on the same probability space.

L2 ⇒ L1 ⇒ probability ⇒ distribution;   almost sure ⇒ probability. Convergence in probability to a constant is equivalent to convergence in distribution to a point mass at that constant.

These implications cannot generally be reversed. Independent events with P(An) = 1/n give indicators converging in probability but not almost surely (second Borel–Cantelli). The spike Xn = n·1[0,1/n] converges almost surely and in probability but not in L2. Enumerating the dyadic intervals gives L2 convergence without almost sure convergence. Finally, Xn = (−1)nX with X ~ N(0,1) always has the same law but does not converge in probability.

Tools. Markov's inequality gives mean-square ⇒ probability; the continuous mapping theorem, Slutsky's theorem and the delta method justify most asymptotic inference. A subsequence converging in probability has an almost surely convergent subsubsequence; Skorokhod's representation lets a distributional limit be realized almost surely on a new space.

2. The LLN, the CLT, and why √n appears

Law of Large Numbers. The weak law says X̄n →P μ (finite mean suffices; Chebyshev gives the bound σ2/(nε2) under finite variance). The strong law says X̄n → μ almost surely. With finite variance the mean-square law adds the exact rate

E[(X̄n − μ)2] = Var(X̄n) = σ2/n,   SE(X̄n) = σ/√n.

The LLN locates the average but says nothing about the shape or size of its fluctuations, nor that short samples must “balance out”.

Central Limit Theorem. For iid variables with finite positive variance,

Zn = √n(X̄n − μ)/σ ⇒ N(0,1).

This is a statement about laws, not about the values Zn settling down: the histogram becomes normal while the variables keep fluctuating.

Where √n comes from. For square-integrable variables,

Var(Sn) = ∑Var(Xi) + 2∑i<j Cov(Xi, Xj).

Independence kills the covariances, so Var(Sn) = nσ2 and SD(Sn) = σ√n. The mean grows like nμ but fluctuations grow only like √n; dividing by σ√n leaves a non-degenerate limit. This single fact gives standard errors, confidence intervals, test statistics, the delta method, MLE asymptotic normality and the Bayesian Bernstein–von Mises approximation. Dependence can inflate the asymptotic variance without changing the √n rate; infinite variance can change the rate entirely — for standard Cauchy data the sample mean is standard Cauchy for every n and never concentrates.

3. Conditional probability and Bayes' theorem

For P(B) > 0, conditional probability renormalises the sample space to B:

P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B)/P(B),   P(A) = ∑i P(A | Bi)P(Bi).

Applying the multiplication rule twice gives Bayes' theorem. For a parameter θ with prior density π(θ) and observed data x,

π(θ | x) = L(θ; x)π(θ)/m(x) ∝ L(θ; x)π(θ),   m(x) = ∫ L(θ; x)π(θ) dθ.

Prior π(θ) is the pre-data uncertainty. Likelihood L(θ; x) = p(x | θ) is a function of θ for fixed data — it is not a probability distribution over θ. Posterior π(θ | x) is the updated distribution; it contains everything needed for Bayesian estimation, credible intervals, prediction and decisions. Marginal likelihood m(x) normalises the posterior and is the evidence in Bayes factors. Posterior ∝ prior × likelihood is all there is: the posterior of one batch becomes the prior for the next, so updating is sequential and order-independent for a fixed model.

Conditional probability is not causation: P(A | B) is an association in the studied population, and a strong association can be reversed by a third variable (Simpson's paradox).

4. Worked examples

Medical diagnosis and the base rate

With prevalence 1%, sensitivity 99% and specificity 95%,

P(D | +) = (0.99 × 0.01)/(0.99 × 0.01 + 0.05 × 0.99) = 0.0099/0.0594 ≈ 0.167.

Only about one in six positive tests is a true case: the healthy group is large. At 10% prevalence the same test gives 0.099/0.144 = 0.6875. The evidence is identical; the prior changes the conclusion. Natural frequencies make this concrete:

Other common conjugate pairs are Normal–Normal (known variance), Gamma–Poisson and Dirichlet–Multinomial.

5. Advantages, limitations, and influence on conclusions

Advantages. Bayes' theorem is coherent updating by the ordinary rules of probability. It gives direct probability statements about parameters, lets prior knowledge and physical constraints enter explicitly, stabilises small samples, supports sequential and hierarchical learning, and provides a unified language for prediction, decisions and model comparison. Gaussian priors give ridge regularisation; a Beta prior acts as pseudo-counts.

Limitations. The posterior is conditional on the prior and the model, not a guarantee of truth. Eliciting a prior is hard; strong or conflicting priors can dominate small samples; improper priors can make marginal likelihoods ill-defined; computation (MCMC, variational inference) can be costly; and a misspecified likelihood can produce confident wrong answers. Bernstein–von Mises says prior influence fades for smooth summaries in regular finite-dimensional models (posterior ≈ N(θ̂n, I(θ̂n)−1/n), but it can fail at boundaries, in high dimensions and for non-identifiable models.

Influence on conclusions. The prior matters most for small samples, tail probabilities, Bayes factors and predictions far from the data; for smooth summaries its weight typically decays like 1/n. Good practice is to report the prior, check sensitivity to reasonable alternatives, run prior predictive checks, and separate robust conclusions from prior-driven ones. Frequentist inference instead fixes the parameter and evaluates procedures over repeated samples: a 95% confidence interval has 95% coverage, while a 95% credible interval contains the parameter with posterior probability 0.95 given the model and prior. A p-value is P(data at least as extreme | H0), not P(H0 | data).

Conclusion

Convergence in distribution is the weakest mode and underlies the CLT; convergence in probability underlies the weak LLN and consistency; almost sure convergence underlies the strong LLN; mean-square convergence underlies MSE and the finite-variance LLN. The LLN locates the average at μ, while the CLT describes fluctuations around it. Variance additivity forces the √n scale that appears in standard errors, intervals and test statistics. Bayes' theorem is conditional probability applied to parameters and data: prior × likelihood, normalised into a posterior. Its conclusions are explicitly conditional on those inputs, which is why sensitivity analysis and honest reporting matter.
