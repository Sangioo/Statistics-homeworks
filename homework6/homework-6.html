Independence and the chi-square test

X and F are independent when

P(X = xi, F = cj) = P(X = xi) P(F = cj) for all i, j,
equivalently P(F = cj | X = xi) = P(F = cj).

H0: X and F are independent. H1: they are dependent.

The expected count under H0, the Pearson statistic, the degrees of freedom and the p-value are

eij = fi· f·j/n;
χ² = ∑i∑j (fij − eij)²/eij;
df = (r − 1)(c − 1), where r and c are the numbers of X and F levels;
p = P(χ²df ≥ χ²obs | H0).

The approximation requires independent observations and adequate expected counts (about 5 or more per cell). A small p-value is evidence against independence, not a measure of effect strength. Report Cramér's V = √(χ² / [n min(r − 1, c − 1)]) as well; rough benchmarks are 0.1, 0.3 and 0.5 for small, medium and large associations.

Interpretation

Association and causation

A significant chi-square is evidence of association, not proof of causation. The data are observational: entry vectors were not assigned, containment times were not controlled, and a third variable could affect both. Failing to reject independence is also not proof of independence, and a small p-value does not imply a strong association.

A causal claim would require a credible design, such as randomization or a natural experiment, temporal ordering, a plausible mechanism, and control of confounders.

Conclusion

For the illustrative sample, χ² = 57.713 with df = 6 and p ≈ 1.31 × 10−10, so we reject independence at the 5% level. Cramér's V ≈ 0.219 indicates a modest association. The seeded control gives χ² = 5.632 and p = 0.4657, consistent with independence.
