A small elliptic curve makes every step visible.

The calculation

Use the toy curve y² = x³ + 7 (mod 17) and base point G = (1, 5). A private key k gives the public point Q = kG. Scalar multiplication means adding G to itself k times, using the elliptic curve group operation.

For points P = (x₁, y₁) and R = (x₂, y₂), the slope is λ = (y₂ − y₁)/(x₂ − x₁) for distinct points, or λ = 3x₁²/(2y₁) when doubling. Then x₃ = λ² − x₁ − x₂ and y₃ = λ(x₁ − x₃) − y₁, all modulo 17. Division here means multiplication by a modular inverse. The point at infinity, O, is the identity; P + (−P) = O.

In this subgroup, x = 1, 2, 5 and 12 each appear twice; every observed y value appears once. The curve equation links x and y. Also, k and 9 − k produce opposite points: they have the same x and opposite y values modulo 17. This is a clear input/output relationship even if a simple numerical correlation looks small.

As an avalanche-like check, compare the 10-bit strings x || y for consecutive keys, from 1 → 2 through 7 → 8. The numbers of changed bits are . A change from k to k + 1 may flip several input bits, so this is only a descriptive comparison of one encoding. Changing exactly one bit of a valid k gives between 2 and 7 changed output bits in this example; there is no rule that half must change.

What matters cryptographically? Scalar multiplication is deterministic and efficient in the forward direction. With a suitable large curve, finding k from Q = kG is believed to be difficult (the elliptic curve discrete logarithm problem). A cryptographic hash instead accepts arbitrary-length input and gives a fixed-length digest; preimage and collision resistance and strong bit diffusion are hash-related goals. Coordinate frequencies and bit counts from eight toy points do not establish security.
